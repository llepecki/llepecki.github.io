### Corrections the app must preserve

- An orbiting station **does fall**. In a circular orbit, its acceleration points inward while its distance from Earth's center remains constant. Acceleration toward Earth does not require that distance to decrease.
- Sideways velocity is motion, not an outward force. In the Earth-centered inertial view, gravity supplies the centripetal acceleration. Do not draw a balancing centrifugal force in that view.
- Velocity is perpendicular to gravity everywhere on a circular orbit. On an ellipse this occurs at the nearest and farthest points, not everywhere.
- Slightly below circular speed can produce an ellipse that still misses Earth. Slightly above circular speed produces a higher ellipse, not immediate escape.
- Escape means an unbound trajectory in this Earth-only model. Gravity continues acting and slows an outward-moving craft. There is no finite boundary where gravity switches off.
- Weightlessness is not restricted to circular or stable orbits. The astronaut also floats during an unpowered direct fall, before impact, when it starts with the same velocity as the cabin.
- An engine does not instantly make a floating astronaut feel a push. The astronaut initially continues freely; a push acts when a wall reaches them. A secured person would feel thrust immediately through their restraint.

These are scientific requirements, not optional teaching details.

## 2. Learning experience

The learner should be able to answer four questions after a few minutes:

1. Why does the station keep missing Earth?
2. What changes when it starts slower or faster?
3. Why can the astronaut float while gravity is strong?
4. Why does the astronaut move relative to the cabin when an engine fires?

Use one exploratory simulator. Its main view is a close view of the station above Earth's visibly curved surface, with a small orbit map for context. A separate, enlarged cabin strip stays below it. These views display the same physical state and simulation time. Avoid an initial menu or a separate game mode.

### First screen

Load the exact circular preset at 400 km, paused. Select **Close view**, with **Follow station** on. Show the cropped, curved Earth surface, the station's position, a green velocity arrow, an inward pink gravity arrow, a blue curved prediction, and a dotted straight continuation labeled **Without gravity**. The default must reveal the falling-around-Earth comparison without opening an inspection tool. A small full-Earth orbit map shows where this local scene sits on the orbit. The cabin contains a centered astronaut.

The first instruction is: **“Press Start. Watch the station fall around Earth.”** A short caption beside the cabin says: **“The station and the astronaut fall together.”** Live readouts distinguish `Gravity here: 8.69 m/s²` from `Wall push on astronaut: 0.00 N`. The default playback selection is 120×; the learner starts it explicitly.

The recommended exploration order is discoverable through the presets and help copy:

| Experiment                  | Learner action                                               | Visible result                                                                                                                  |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Continuous fall             | Start the circular preset; optionally press Show the fall    | The path bends away from a straight tangent above curved Earth. Inspection pauses and steps this comparison from new positions. |
| Insufficient sideways speed | Choose Too slow, then Start                                  | The trajectory intersects Earth; the floating astronaut remains in free fall until impact.                                      |
| A circle is one case        | Compare Lower ellipse, Circular, and Higher ellipse          | Altitude stays constant only for the circle. The orbit map shows the different overall paths.                                   |
| Escape                      | Choose Escape                                                | The orbit map shows an open path. Gravity still points toward Earth even when the surface leaves the close view.                |
| Weight during thrust        | Return to Circular; press Speed up                           | Playback switches to 1×. The cabin advances relative to the astronaut; a wall eventually pushes them.                           |
| Braking and coasting        | Reset the astronaut; press Brake, then stop the engine early | The astronaut moves toward the opposite wall and retains its relative velocity after the engine stops.                          |

**Show the fall** adds a paused, annotated comparison and a step control; it is not the first place Earth's curvature becomes visible. **Orbit map** expands the overview into the upper region when the learner wants the whole trajectory. Neither requires a separate lesson mode. The cabin remains visible in both views.

## 4. Controls and behavior

Panel order: preset → initial speed → playback → primary Start/Pause action → engines → explanation/view tools → additional readouts → collapsed help and sources.

### Initial conditions

Starting altitude is fixed at 400 km in version one. In world coordinates, start above Earth's center with rightward tangential velocity. The close camera frames that position above a cropped curved surface, not above a fully visible globe. This makes “sideways” obvious and keeps every speed comparison at the same height.

### Playback

- Retain the reference's `▶`, `▶▶`, `▶▶▶`, and `■` glyphs. Their rates are **1×, 120×, and 600×** simulation seconds per real second. Show those values and include them in localized accessible labels.
- Start runs at the selected rate. While running, the primary button becomes Pause; after pausing it becomes Continue. The active playback glyph becomes `▮▮`.
- Selecting another rate starts or continues at that rate; selecting the active playing glyph pauses. All these controls call the same state transition functions.
- `■` resets the current initial conditions and astronaut to time zero, stops engines, restores the default Close view with Follow station on, and pauses. It does not revert a custom speed to the circular preset. Preset changes and initial-speed edits use the same camera reset.
- A clock reports simulated elapsed time. It is distinct from the selected playback multiplier.
- At 120×, the default circular orbit takes about 46 real seconds. At 600× it takes about 9.2 seconds. Never change physical speed to implement playback speed.

### Live engine demonstration

**Decided by the Captain on 2026-10-07:** Speed up and Brake are hold-to-fire engine domes in the style of the Hohmann app's burn button. Thrust lasts while a dome is held (pointer press, or a held Space/Enter on the focused dome) and stops on release, pointer cancel, focus loss, window blur, pause, Escape, impact, reset and every other clock interruption. There is no fixed four-second pulse, no "Tap again to stop", no remaining-time readout and no separate Turn engines off button. The burn runs at the selected playback rate (the 1× rule below no longer applies), its acceleration ramps with real hold time, and the accumulated delta-v readout still applies; see the implementation record in section 9. The paragraphs that follow describe the original tap-to-pulse design and are kept for the physics they state.


Use three large controls: **Speed up**, **Brake**, **Engines off**. A tap on either firing control starts a **four-second simulation-time pulse at 0.5 m/s²**. Tapping the active control again stops it early. Tapping the other firing control reverses the command and starts a fresh four-second pulse. Only one can be active.

This tap interaction works with native Enter/Space activation and touch; a held input is not required. Display “Tap again to stop” during a pulse, the remaining duration, and the accumulated thrust delta-v.

Starting a pulse from a paused or running experiment sets the **entire simulation to 1×**, closes the falling-path inspection, selects Close view with Follow station on at its default scale, and runs it. Disable 120× and 600× while thrust is active. At cutoff, keep 1× selected and continue coasting so the learner can inspect the astronaut. A manual Pause cuts thrust immediately and freezes both views; Continue does not re-arm it.

Apply acceleration parallel or opposite to the **current** Earth-frame velocity. The cabin canvas shows four fixed engines, one at the middle of each wall; a controller splits the commanded acceleration onto them (each engine's thrust is the positive part of the component along its push direction), so the two active engines' flames change continuously as the velocity direction turns with the orbit. The orbit-view marker shows no exhaust. This represents a simplified steerable thruster array, not a rotating ISS model. The square cabin keeps a fixed orientation relative to distant stars.

If speed is below 1 m/s, disable velocity-directed engines with an explanation that a direction of motion is not yet defined reliably. A braking substep must stop thrust before thrust alone would reverse the velocity; do not divide by zero or alternate direction around zero.

The pulse adds up to 2 m/s of thrust delta-v. Its effect on a roughly 7,673 m/s orbital speed is intentionally small. The caption says: **“Short burns show what happens inside. Use starting speed to compare very different orbits.”** The live prediction still updates from the changed state. Do not magnify the actual orbital impulse to make it more conspicuous.

## 5. Make continuous falling visible

### Default close view: real curvature, not a flat horizon

The physical model is Earth-centered and nonrotating. The default drawing is a **cropped, close view** of that model: the station above a visibly curved segment of Earth's true circular surface. A full globe is context in the inset, not the main teaching view.

Use an isotropic world-to-screen scale: Earth's radius, station altitude, path curvature, and straight comparison share the same meters per pixel. Never flatten Earth into a floor, exaggerate its curvature, compress altitude, or bend the path independently. Enlarge only the station icon, vectors' display lengths, and the separately labeled cabin. Mark the station's actual position with a small dot and an offset leader if needed; collision detection never uses icon size.

At the initial 400 km height, begin with a horizontal world span near **3,000 km**. Place the station roughly 30% across and 48% down the main drawing, leaving space ahead for the comparison and above/right for the map. Adjust framing for the actual aspect ratio and reserved map rectangle. Keep the nearest surface visible with at least 24 px of Earth below it. At a 320 px wide initial drawing, require at least **32 CSS px between the station's center and the nearest surface** and **12 CSS px of surface bow relative to a chord across the visible arc**. These are readability tests, not permission to alter geometry. Resize/grow the drawing or move the inset before reducing readability.

The close camera follows the station and rotates the **display** so the instantaneous inward radial direction is down. At the circular start, velocity points right. Use one current camera translation, rotation, and scale for every world point in a rendered frame: Earth, the actual trail, future samples, reference arc, and straight continuation. Never transform each prediction sample using its own future camera position; that would erase the bend being taught. Retain trail points in world coordinates, not old screen coordinates.

The velocity arrow is the actual **Earth-relative velocity**, rotated into the display; it is not velocity after subtracting camera motion. The gravity arrow uses the same display rotation and always points toward Earth's center. Their lengths have separate labeled display scales because velocity and acceleration have different units. No right-angle marker is drawn (dropped by the Captain on 2026-10-07 as noise; `explainPerpendicular` carries the idea in words).

Camera movement never changes forces, station attitude, or cabin physics. The cabin strip stays in the nonrotating translating frame specified in section 6, with its own changing Earth-direction indicator. If the main station glyph depicts orientation, transform its fixed inertial attitude with the close camera; do not physically rotate the station to match the camera. Explain the camera turn in help. Background stars are subdued decoration, not a second physical reference that changes the solver.

Show altitude as a radial measurement from the real surface, not distance from Earth's center. At circular conditions it remains constant. The overview shows velocity changing direction around Earth; in the turning close camera it may remain screen-right. Never treat that screen direction as evidence that velocity is constant in the physical frame.

Rendering order within each separately clipped view: background → predicted path → Earth disk masking interior path segments → actual trail → station icon → vector arrows → station position dot → labels (the arrows and the dot sit on top of the opaque icon, decided by the Captain on 2026-10-07). Stop prediction and trail at the first surface intersection. No path through Earth and no engine exhaust during unpowered flight.

The explanation is: **“As the station moves sideways, Earth curves away below it. At circular speed, gravity bends its path into a circle around Earth.”** Expanded physics shows `v² / r = MU_EARTH / r²`: the inward acceleration needed for that circular path equals gravity's acceleration. This is one acceleration, not a balance of two forces.

### Orbit map: context and optional expanded view

Keep a small Earth-centered, nonrotating map visible in Close view. It draws the full Earth disk, actual station position, finite predicted path, and a locator showing the part of the world covered by the close camera. Clip the locator to the map and do not imply that its dimensions are the physical size of the enlarged station. Both views use the same state and solver output, but have separate spatial scales.

Selecting **Orbit map** expands this overview into the upper canvas. Retain the separate cabin strip, gravity/contact readouts, and all controls. Selecting **Close view** returns to the detailed view. Neither action advances time, resets a trajectory, changes thrust, or creates a second simulation.

Fit ordinary closed orbits when practical. In expanded Auto framing, retain an Earth disk radius of at least 48 px where space permits; that minimum does not apply to the small inset. Near escape, a bound ellipse can be enormous. Show a finite segment and a labeled continuation arrow rather than forcing the whole conic into the viewport. Offscreen station markers include direction and altitude; the full physical globe need not share the close view's detail.

### Continuous comparison and “Show the fall” inspection

During ordinary unpowered Close view, draw a rolling comparison from the current station state, normally over the next **120 simulated seconds**:

1. **Straight continuation:** `r_straight(t) = r_start + v_start * t`, a dotted neutral line labeled **Without gravity**.
2. **Actual free fall:** an unpowered clone of that same state advanced by the solver, long-dashed blue and labeled **With gravity**.
3. Both predictions start at the same point, use the same elapsed times and camera transform, and do not advance the live state.

If the physical prediction hits Earth sooner, truncate both comparisons at that impact time. At zero speed, the straight-continuation marker stays in place. During engine firing, hide the straight comparison and label the coast prediction **Path if engines stop now**; it is not the actual powered future. Restore the rolling free-fall comparison at cutoff.

**Show the fall** freezes this existing comparison, keeps the curved Earth surface and orbit map visible, and adds:

- Endpoint markers and a connector labeled **Change caused by gravity**. This connector is a displacement, not a force arrow.
- A thin starting-distance reference arc at `|r_start|`, centered on Earth. Only the circular example stays on that arc throughout.
- The equal comparison duration and the **Next step (+120 s)** control.

Use the default close scale or a tighter isotropic framing only if it still contains the true nearby surface and all relevant comparison geometry. Never replace the surface with a flat line or an unrelated decorative arc. At the circular start, 120 seconds gives about 921 km of straight-line travel and about 63 km of gravitational deviation. Those real distances make the distinction readable; endpoint labels and the connector help at smaller sizes without magnifying one displacement separately.

Next step advances the actual unpowered station, astronaut, and clock and repeats the comparison from the new position. Mark the last three starting points in the orbit map so the learner sees that “toward Earth” changes as the station moves. During each paused comparison use one frozen camera transform; when stepping, reframe at the new origin without hiding the real motion on the map.

The **Without gravity** line is a hypothetical comparison, not a second real object. It never switches off gravity in the live world or cabin.

### Distant trajectories and camera limits

Close view does not automatically zoom out to fit a whole ellipse or escape path. Follow the station at the chosen local scale. When the real surface leaves the crop, show **Earth's surface outside this view**, an Earth-direction indicator, and altitude. The persistent map provides the global context; the learner may expand it. Do not draw a nearby false horizon just to keep Earth below the craft. For an outward unbound trajectory, pause at `r = 20 * r0` and report **“Continuing outward beyond this view. Gravity still acts.”** This is a demonstration endpoint, not the edge of gravity. Classify escape from energy before reaching it. Do not terminate a bound orbit as escape when it goes offscreen.

## 6. Physical model for implementation

All equations here use SI units unless a displayed unit is explicitly given. The formulas are standard Newtonian mechanics; numerical thresholds and UI tolerances are independently derived for this design.

### Constants and station motion

```text
R_EARTH       = 6_371_000 m
MU_EARTH      = 3.98600435507e14 m³/s²
START_ALTITUDE = 400_000 m
r0            = R_EARTH + START_ALTITUDE
g_standard    = 9.80665 m/s²  (only for optional “g” units)

initial position = (0, r0)
initial velocity = (v_start, 0)

g_vector(r) = -MU_EARTH * r_vector / |r_vector|³
dr/dt = v
dv/dt = g_vector(r) + engine_acceleration

local_gravity = MU_EARTH / |r|²
altitude = |r| - R_EARTH
v_circular(r) = sqrt(MU_EARTH / |r|)
v_escape(r) = sqrt(2 * MU_EARTH / |r|)
```

Convert to screen coordinates only in rendering; world y points upward and canvas y points downward. Use `MU_EARTH` directly rather than multiplying separately rounded mass and gravitational-constant values.

No atmospheric drag, Earth rotation or oblateness, terrain, Moon, Sun, or mutual station–astronaut gravity is simulated. Show a persistent compact `Vacuum model` label and put the full limitations in help. At very low altitudes, state that a real spacecraft would encounter atmospheric drag before the modeled surface impact. Do not render a realistic fiery reentry from this model.

### Orbit classification

```text
specific_energy = dot(v, v) / 2 - MU_EARTH / |r|
h_vector = cross(r, v)                    (embed 2D vectors in 3D)
e_vector = cross(v, h_vector) / MU_EARTH - r / |r|
eccentricity = |e_vector|
p = |h_vector|² / MU_EARTH
perigee_radius = p / (1 + eccentricity)

For specific_energy < 0:
  semi_major_axis = -MU_EARTH / (2 * specific_energy)
  apogee_radius = semi_major_axis * (1 + eccentricity)
  period = 2 * pi * sqrt(semi_major_axis³ / MU_EARTH)
```

Process actual collision first. Handle radial motion explicitly before using formulas that divide by angular momentum or require a periapsis direction. In particular, the zero-speed drop has eccentricity 1 and **negative energy**: it is not a parabolic escape.

For future outcomes, check whether the future unpowered branch intersects Earth. A bound ellipse with perigee at or below the surface will intersect; an already outbound unbound trajectory can have a mathematical periapsis in its past, so use radial direction and the future branch rather than applying the perigee test blindly.

Then classify collision-free motion by energy and eccentricity, checking the near-zero band first:

- Near-zero energy: `Escape threshold` within `1e-8 * (MU_EARTH / |r|)` J/kg. Keep this tolerance separate from scientific equality and never snap velocity or energy to achieve it.
- Negative energy outside that band: ellipse; `e < 1e-5` may be labeled `Nearly circular` for display. The exact default preset is `Circular`.
- Positive energy outside that band: `Escape trajectory`.

During thrust, label the prediction **“Path if engines stop now.”** Compute it from the current state. Do not draw an unchanging pre-burn conic as the future trajectory.

For the initial tangential release below circular speed, a useful independent boundary is:

```text
v_surface_graze = sqrt(2 * MU_EARTH * R_EARTH / (r0 * (r0 + R_EARTH)))
                ≈ 7.554932 km/s
```

Derivation: set the initial point to apogee `r0`, set perigee to `R_EARTH`, use `a = (r0 + R_EARTH) / 2`, then apply `v² = MU_EARTH * (2/r0 - 1/a)`. At this initial height, speeds between this boundary and circular speed produce lower ellipses that clear the solid surface in the vacuum model. Equality touches the surface and counts as impact.

### Integration and events

Use a deterministic RK4 station integrator with fixed steps no larger than one simulated second during unpowered flight. Engine burns use substeps no larger than 1/120 second, evaluating velocity-directed thrust consistently at the RK stages. Split a step at the exact pulse cutoff.

Playback controls the amount of simulated time consumed, not the step size. Clamp frame elapsed time to 0.05 seconds, use an accumulator, bound work per rendered frame, and carry unfinished simulation time forward without dropping physical steps. If sustained load requires slowing playback, report the effective rate rather than corrupting time or increasing integration steps.

Handle astronaut–wall contact with continuous crossing detection within each coast interval; under zero relative acceleration its time to a wall is analytic. During thrust, refine contact within the short substep. Station surface events also require root refinement, including grazing contact or a within-step minimum that touches Earth. Do not rely solely on the endpoint being underground. Stop at the first surface event and pin the position to the boundary.

Use one event time for station, cabin, clock, and trail. After surface impact, freeze the scene, stop engines, and report the outcome. Post-impact structural damage and astronaut motion are outside the model.

When the page is hidden or loses focus, cut thrust and pause. On return, remain paused with no catch-up interval. Resizing, changing language, and zooming never advance physics.

### The cabin and astronaut

The cabin is a 4.00 m × 5.71 m rectangle (walls at `x = ±2 m` and `y = ±2.857 m`, width-to-height ratio 0.7) drawn with the same proportions as the station marker. The body inside it is a symbolic astronaut: a stick figure 1.70 m tall and 0.60 m wide (half-extents `0.30 m × 0.85 m`) with mass `70 kg`, drawn upright on screen (feet toward the bottom wall). Its center starts at the cabin center with zero velocity relative to the cabin. The astronaut's mass has no effect on free-fall acceleration; treat it as negligible compared with the station and ignore the station's recoil from wall contact.

Show one pink gravitational-acceleration arrow on the astronaut; the pink "Gravity here" readout beside the canvas carries the value. Keep it visible while the astronaut floats. Draw the distinct white contact arrow only when a wall actually pushes the astronaut. The cabin shows no other vectors: the engine burn is shown by the wall engines' flames.

Use a **nonrotating frame translating with the station**, with axes fixed relative to the Earth-centered inertial axes. No torque acts in this model, so neither the station nor the astronaut turns: no wall is a floor (the bottom wall points at Earth only at the start), and the gravity arrows swing once per orbit while the figure stays upright on screen. Rotating the cabin to keep a wall facing Earth or to align with velocity would introduce rotational inertial terms absent from this simplified model.

Across this small cabin, approximate Earth's gravitational acceleration as uniform. Ignore tidal gradients and cabin air currents. In this approximation:

```text
astronaut_position = station_position + q
astronaut_velocity = station_velocity + u

station_acceleration = gravity + engine_acceleration
astronaut_acceleration = gravity + contact_force / astronaut_mass

dq/dt = u
du/dt = -engine_acceleration + contact_force / astronaut_mass
```

This subtraction explains the relative motion; it does not remove gravity from the inertial world. The local approximation deliberately omits small real microgravity disturbances. Do not introduce an artificial spring, random floating animation, or velocity damping to keep the astronaut centered.

Use frictionless, fully inelastic normal wall contact for a clear demonstration. At contact, resolve only the velocity component into the wall; preserve tangential velocity. While thrust presses the astronaut into a wall, apply the minimum inward normal force needed to prevent penetration. A wall can push but never pull. Release contact immediately when the unconstrained relative acceleration or velocity points away. Resolve both normals at a corner. The center's travel limits are the cabin half-sizes minus the body half-extents: `1.70 m` sideways and `2.007 m` toward the top or bottom wall.

For a sustained contact with inward unit normal `n` and zero normal relative velocity:

```text
N = astronaut_mass * max(0, dot(engine_acceleration, n))
```

Away from walls, `N = 0`, even when the engines are firing. Impact is a brief impulse; display `Brief contact` at that event, not an invented finite peak force. The steady wall-push readout can show Newtons and, in expanded details, `N / (astronaut_mass * g_standard)` in g units.

Starting at the top of the circular orbit, Speed up accelerates the cabin rightward. The astronaut approaches the left wall. Brake produces the opposite relative motion. At `0.5 m/s²`, an initially centered astronaut reaches a side wall after `sqrt(2 * 1.70 / 0.5) = 2.608 s` and would reach the top or bottom wall after `2.834 s`. With a roughly wall-normal acceleration, the subsequent push is `70 kg × 0.5 m/s² = 35 N`, or `0.051 g`; at the 300 m/s² throttle cap it is 21,000 N, about 30.6 g. Because the thrust direction turns with the orbit while the cabin does not, a long burn also slides a supported astronaut slowly along the wall toward a corner. These are demonstration values, not ISS reboost specifications.

If an engine stops before contact, the astronaut continues drifting. If it stops while the astronaut is already resting against a wall, the support force becomes zero; in this ideal model the astronaut may remain touching the wall without being pressed against it. Position alone does not imply weight.

## 9. Implementation organization

Keep one self-contained `index.html`, one inline stylesheet, and one inline script inside a strict IIFE. No sidecar runtime files, Jekyll front matter, or remote physics services. Follow repository metadata, canonical URL, translation, and review-comment conventions.

Organize the code into these responsibilities; names are descriptive suggestions:

| Responsibility        | Inputs and outputs                                                                                             |
| --------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Constants and presets | Source-backed constants and exact initial values                                                               |
| Station physics       | State + duration + thrust → new state and events                                                               |
| Orbital elements      | Position and velocity → energy, conic, apsides, classification                                                 |
| Cabin physics         | Relative astronaut state + shared thrust history + duration → contact and motion                               |
| Prediction            | Copy of station state → future unpowered path; never mutates live state                                        |
| Clock and actions     | Start/pause/reset/rate/pulse events → one coherent runtime state                                               |
| Rendering             | State → close view or expanded orbit map, context inset, cabin canvas; camera transforms cannot mutate physics |
| UI and translations   | State → DOM labels, localized numbers, accessibility summaries                                                 |

Store station position and velocity, astronaut offset and relative velocity, clock, phase, initial preset/speed, thrust/pulse state, classification, `viewMode`, separate close/map camera scales, close-camera transform/follow setting, inspection snapshot, and language explicitly. Keep rendering scales out of physics. The cabin solver receives the same engine acceleration used by the station solver; do not reconstruct it from an animation flag.

Recompute path predictions when initial conditions change, when a burn changes the trajectory, or when required for the visible segment. During unpowered flight, reuse the conic and advance its visible portion. Bound sampling and trail history. Use an analytic conic with adaptive drawing density or a validated forward prediction; handle radial, circular, and near-parabolic cases without divisions by zero. Never draw a closed ellipse at a uniform angular speed as the physical motion.

### Implementation record (2026-10-07)

The shipped `index.html` follows this design with the following concrete choices; each stays within the stated constraints and is listed here so a later pass does not treat it as drift.

- **Step grid.** Unpowered flight integrates in fixed 1 s RK4 steps at 120× and 600×, and in fixed 1/120 s steps at 1× (the rate every engine pulse forces), so the step sequence never depends on frame timing. Thrust substeps are 1/120 s with velocity-directed acceleration evaluated at every RK stage; the cabin receives the stage-weighted engine acceleration from the same step. A frame consumes whole steps from an accumulator (frame time clamped to 0.05 s, work capped at about 12 ms per frame, backlog capped at two seconds of the selected rate); the measured effective rate feeds `labelEffectivePlayback` and `hintPlaybackSlower`.
- **Surface events.** The crossing is bisected inside the step (40 iterations) and a within-step radial minimum is checked whenever the radial velocity changes sign, so a grazing pass that dips below the surface between two above-surface samples is still an impact. - **Classification thresholds.** `Circular` for `e < 1e-7`, `Nearly circular` for `e < 1e-5`, radial when `|h|` is below `1e-6` of the local circular angular momentum, the escape-threshold band as specified. The exact circular preset keeps `e` around `1e-14` across ten orbits.

### 12.1 Translation and wording rules

- Use the same term for the same concept. Use “astronaut” / “astronauta” and “station” / “stacja” throughout, rather than alternating with “body,” “particle,” or “vehicle” in basic explanations.
- Use “speed” for a numeric value and “velocity” when direction matters. In familiar Polish controls, “prędkość” is appropriate for the speed readout. When teaching the distinction, explain “szybkość” as “jak szybko” and velocity as “jak szybko i w którą stronę.” Reserve “wektor prędkości” for expanded physics.
- An acceleration can change speed, direction, or both. Do not translate every occurrence of “acceleration” as “moving faster.”
- Use “wall push” / “nacisk ściany” for the contact-force readout. Its unit is N, not Pa. Avoid an unexplained “weight” / “ciężar” label that could mean either gravity's pull or the push felt from a support.
- Use “weightlessness” / “nieważkość,” never “no gravity” / “brak grawitacji,” for the cabin experience. “Without gravity” is permitted only on the explicitly hypothetical comparison line.
- Distinguish commands from states: “Engines off” / “Silniki wyłączone” is a state; the engine domes are held, not toggled, so their hints say “hold” and “release”.
- Distinguish playback from physical motion: “Playback speed” / “Tempo odtwarzania” changes how quickly time is shown. “Initial sideways speed” / “Początkowa prędkość w bok” changes the starting experiment.
- Use “press” / “naciśnij” for input that works with mouse, touch, and keyboard. Keep labels short, but do not shorten away a scientifically necessary qualification.
- Translate complete sentences. Do not build explanations by joining translated nouns, verbs, and directions. Preserve all named placeholders exactly; their order may change in Polish.
- Use the current-state message that matches the physics. A drifting astronaut can still be in free fall; contact with a wall does not imply a sustained push after the engine stops.

Use `Intl.NumberFormat` with the selected language, not a hard-coded locale. For example, the same value appears as `7.673 km/s` in English and `7,673 km/s` in Polish; `8,558 km` in English uses a grouping space in Polish. Keep a nonbreaking space between a value and its unit. Unit symbols are case-sensitive: `N` is a newton; `m` is a meter. In `valueStandardGravity`, `g` is a multiple of standard gravitational acceleration, not grams; keep this optional display in expanded physics and explain it there.

`{value}` and `{rate}` contain localized numbers without units. `{duration}`, `{speed}`, `{altitude}`, `{gravity}`, `{wallPush}`, `{time}`, and `{acceleration}` contain already formatted quantities including units. For `explainStandardGravityUnit`, format the defined standard value `9.80665 m/s²`, not the local gravity. `{path}`, `{preset}`, `{engineState}`, and `{ballState}` contain the corresponding translated labels. Substitute each named token after choosing the language; do not translate or alter token names. Templates avoid attaching a Polish adjective or verb form to an arbitrary inserted noun.

Use scientific precision internally and rounded values only for display. Preserve `≈` for rounded exact circular/escape presets. Show `—` plus the applicable explanation for unavailable values; never show `NaN`, `Infinity`, an empty label, or an untranslated key to a child.

### 12.10 Wording to avoid

| Avoid                                                                                                       | Reason                                                      | Use instead                                                                |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| “There is no gravity in the station.” / “W stacji nie ma grawitacji.”                                       | Confuses weightlessness with an absent gravitational field. | `explainGravityPresent` and `explainFallingTogether`.                      |
| “Speed balances gravity.” / “Prędkość równoważy grawitację.”                                                | Treats velocity as a force.                                 | `explainOrbit` and `explainVelocityNotForce`.                              |
| “Slower than circular speed means a crash.” / “Prędkość mniejsza od kołowej oznacza zderzenie.”             | Excludes valid lower ellipses.                              | `explainLowerEllipse`.                                                     |
| “The station escapes gravity.” / “Stacja ucieka od grawitacji.”                                             | Suggests gravity stops acting.                              | `explainEscape`.                                                           |
| “The engine pulls the astronaut backward.” / “Silnik ciągnie astronautę do tyłu.”                           | Applies thrust directly to a freely floating object.        | `explainEngineFreeBall`, then the appropriate relative-motion explanation. |
| “The astronaut stops when the engine stops.” / “Astronauta zatrzymuje się po wyłączeniu silnika.”           | Erases the astronaut's existing relative motion.            | `explainEngineOffDrift` when the astronaut is drifting.                    |
| “Press Stop” when the control also resets everything. / “Naciśnij Stop,” gdy sterowanie zeruje eksperyment. | Hides a reset behind a pause-like instruction.              | `actionResetExperiment` or `ariaReset`.                                    |

Before implementation is considered complete, check dictionary-key uniqueness, equal placeholder sets in EN and PL, localized number formatting, actual visible label lengths, and every state-specific sentence against its triggering physics. No learner-facing text should be added only in one language.
